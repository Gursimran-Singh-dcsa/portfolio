One of them was <strong>&quot;hamilton furniture movers&quot;</strong>. After a blog page and a steady run of social media posts, <a href="https://movematerelocations.co.nz">Move Mate Relocations</a> now appears on the first page of Google for that keyword.</p>
<p>It didn&#39;t look that simple at the start. This is the story of why the keyword was so hard, what we changed, and the pointers I would pass on to anyone with a similar problem.</p>
<h2>The brief: keywords from the client</h2>
<p>Move Mate Relocations is a New Zealand moving company offering house, office, warehouse and intercity relocation. When I worked on their website, the client had a clear wish: they wanted to show up for a specific set of search terms, because those are the words their customers type when they need a mover.</p>
<p>That&#39;s a reasonable request. It&#39;s also where the real work begins, because a keyword is not something you can simply &quot;add&quot; to a website.</p>
<p><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="/projects/movemate-desktop.jpg" alt="Home page of the Move Mate Relocations NZ website"></p>
<h2>Why these keywords were so hard</h2>
<p>There were two problems.</p>
<p><strong>The keywords were very generic.</strong> Phrases like &quot;furniture movers&quot; plus a city name are what almost every moving company in that city wants to rank for. Many businesses already target them, and Google has plenty of established pages to choose from.</p>
<p><strong>One keyword was another website.</strong> &quot;Hamilton furniture movers&quot; wasn&#39;t just a phrase people search for. It was also the name of an existing website. When a search term matches a site&#39;s own name, that site starts with a big advantage, because the site&#39;s name and its pages line up with exactly what the searcher typed. Beating a result like that with a normal page is difficult.</p>
<p>So we had a client who needed a result, a keyword where everyone was competing, and one result that seemed to own the exact phrase.</p>
<h2>What we did</h2>
<p>We didn&#39;t look for a trick. We decided to build up the website&#39;s relevance and visibility for that phrase in two ways.</p>
<h3>1. We started posting on social media</h3>
<p>We added social media posts for the business. Move Mate has a presence on Facebook and Instagram, and the posts kept the business and its services in front of real people in the area.</p>
<p>I want to be honest here: social media posts are not a direct ranking factor on their own. What they do is make the brand more visible, give people a reason to visit and search for the business, and give the website more places to be mentioned. We used them as support for the main change below, not as a replacement for it.</p>
<h3>2. We added a new blogging page with location guides</h3>
<p>The bigger change was a dedicated blog on the website. Instead of hoping the home page would rank for every city, we created a <strong>blog hub with a page for each location</strong>: Hamilton, Tauranga, Huntly and Auckland furniture movers.</p>
<p>Each location page is built around one clear idea. The Hamilton page is called &quot;Hamilton Furniture Movers: Reliable Local and Intercity Relocation&quot;, and its description explains how Move Mate helps Hamilton families and businesses with careful furniture moving, packing and stress-free delivery. The title, the heading and the description all say what the page is about, in plain words, so both people and search engines understand it straight away.</p>
<p>That structure matters. A page that exists only for &quot;hamilton furniture movers&quot; has a much stronger case to rank for it than a general services page that mentions Hamilton once.</p>
<h2>The result</h2>
<p><strong>Move Mate Relocations reached the first page of Google results for &quot;hamilton furniture movers&quot;.</strong></p>
<p>I can&#39;t tell you which single change did it, and I&#39;d be suspicious of anyone who claims they can. SEO depends on many signals at once. What I can say is that the result came after we added the blog with dedicated location pages and began posting on social media, and that the combination moved a keyword that looked out of reach.</p>
<h2>Pointers if you&#39;re facing the same problem</h2>
<p>These are general lessons from this project, not a full list of everything we did.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Search the keyword yourself first.</strong> Look at who ranks and why. Is it a directory, a big brand, or, as in our case, a site whose name is the keyword? You can&#39;t plan a way in until you know what you&#39;re up against.</li>
<li><strong>Give every important keyword its own page.</strong> One page, one clear topic. Make the title, the main heading and the description say the same thing.</li>
<li><strong>Write for the customer&#39;s question, not just for the keyword.</strong> Explain the service, the area you cover and what makes the move easier. Pages that help people tend to keep them reading.</li>
<li><strong>Add location pages, not just one &quot;services&quot; page.</strong> If you serve several cities, a page per city lets each one compete on its own.</li>
<li><strong>Support your pages with social posts.</strong> Use them to point people to your content and to keep the brand visible, but don&#39;t expect social posts alone to change your rankings.</li>
<li><strong>Get the basics right.</strong> Make sure pages can be crawled, load quickly on mobile and are listed in your sitemap. Move Mate&#39;s site is built with Next.js, which makes this straightforward.</li>
<li><strong>Measure and be patient.</strong> Use Google Search Console to watch which searches bring people in, and give changes time to take effect.</li>
<li><strong>Earn the result with useful content.</strong> Don&#39;t try to imitate a competitor&#39;s name. A better, clearer page is the safer way to win.</li>
</ol>
<h2>What I took away</h2>
<p>The biggest lesson: <strong>a &quot;very hard&quot; keyword is usually a question of focus.</strong> Once the site had a page that was clearly about Hamilton furniture movers, backed by a business that was visible online, the keyword stopped looking impossible.</p>
<p>It also reminded me that SEO is a team job. The client knows the customers, a developer builds the structure, and the content and the social posts have to work together.</p>

And if you&#39;d like help with something similar, message me on WhatsApp.</p>

<h3>Can you rank for a keyword that is another website&#39;s name?</h3>
<p>It&#39;s harder, but it&#39;s possible. For &quot;hamilton furniture movers&quot;, we reached the first page by building a dedicated page for the keyword and supporting it with social media posts.</p>
<h3>What was the main change that helped?</h3>
<p>A new blog with its own page for each location, including a Hamilton furniture movers page. Social media posts supported it.</p>
<h3>Do social media posts improve Google rankings?</h3>
<p>Not directly. They increase visibility and send people to your site, which supports your other SEO work.</p>
