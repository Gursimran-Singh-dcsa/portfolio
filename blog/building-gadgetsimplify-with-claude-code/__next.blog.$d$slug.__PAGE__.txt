<p>If you work in tech, you hear this question all the time. And the funny part is that tech people ask it too. When a new phone, laptop or pair of earbuds is due, even we end up with twenty browser tabs open, a spec sheet we only half understand, and reviews that all end with &quot;it depends.&quot;</p>
<p>I wanted to fix that one small problem. So I built <a href="https://gadgetsimplify.in">GadgetSimplify</a>, and I built it in <strong>under two hours</strong> using Claude Code.</p>
<h2>The problem: too much information, no decision</h2>
<p>Spec sheets tell you what a gadget <em>has</em>. Reviews tell you what a reviewer <em>felt</em>. Neither one tells you the thing you actually want to know: <strong>should I buy this?</strong></p>
<p>Choosing a gadget isn&#39;t hard because information is missing. It&#39;s hard because there&#39;s too much of it, written in language that assumes you already know what a &quot;good&quot; number is.</p>
<h2>The idea: explain it simply, then give a verdict</h2>
<p>GadgetSimplify has one job: explain gadgets in simple language, so you can understand the features that matter and make a smarter buying decision.</p>
<p>The part I care about most is a <strong>verdict section on every review page</strong>. After the explanation, the page tells you plainly <strong>when you should buy this gadget</strong>. You read it, compare it with your own situation, and decide. That&#39;s it. No scoring spreadsheet, no fence-sitting.</p>
<p><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="/projects/gadgetsimplify-desktop.jpg" alt="GadgetSimplify home page with the headline &quot;Technology, Simplified.&quot; and topic cards for smartphones and buying guides"></p>
<h2>Building it with Claude Code</h2>
<p>I had the idea, and I wanted to see how far I could get without turning it into a long project. With Claude Code, I went from an idea to a live website in under two hours.</p>
<p>Here is the shape of what I ended up with:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Next.js</strong> for the site itself.</li>
<li><strong>No database.</strong> All the content, the articles and categories, lives in plain <strong>JSON files</strong>. A site like this doesn&#39;t need a database: there&#39;s nothing to log in to, nothing to migrate and nothing to keep running.</li>
<li><strong>Cloudflare</strong> for deployment, so the site is fast and there is very little to maintain.</li>
</ul>
<p>That last point matters more than it sounds. Because the content is just files, adding a new gadget means adding a new entry, and the site rebuilds. The simplicity of the setup is a big part of why two hours was enough.</p>
<h2>A note on affiliate links</h2>
<p>I use affiliate links on some products. That means if you buy something through one of those links, I may earn a commission. The verdict on a page is written to help you decide, whether or not a link is there.</p>
<h2>What I took away from it</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Start from a real problem.</strong> I didn&#39;t start with &quot;let me build a website.&quot; I started with a question that I and everyone around me keep asking.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the scope small.</strong> One idea, explained simply, with one feature that does the heavy lifting: the verdict.</li>
<li><strong>Pick boring, simple tools.</strong> Files instead of a database and static hosting instead of servers meant less to build and less to break.</li>
<li><strong>AI makes the first version cheap.</strong> The hard part is no longer getting something live. It&#39;s deciding what is worth building.</li>
</ul>
